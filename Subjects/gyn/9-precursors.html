Overview

  • Epithelial cell abnormalities (ECA) are divided into three main categories in TBS
    1. Squamous lesions
    2. Glandular lesions
    3. Other types
  • TBS ranks ECAs in order of clinical significance- triaging of patients from least aggressive to most aggressive
  • Of the squamous lesions:
    • ASCUS is the lowest clinically significant
    • LSIL
    • HSIL
    • Invasive squamous carcinoma is most clinically significant (highest risk of disease and death)
  • All lesions should have classic appearances and fall into well-defined categories
  • There are lesions however that do not (in between patterns)
  • Often cells will demonstrate overlapping criteria or they are small in number
  • These "equivocal/uncertain" findings led to the formation of the atypical cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS) category
  • Before TBS other terms were used:
    • Marked squamous atypia
    • Atypical cellular changes
    • Parakeratosis and atypical parakeratosis
    • Dyskeratosis (still used)
  • Did not lead to good patient outcomes
  • ASCUS was an attempt to standardize these categories and their criteria
  • Remains the category of uncertainty
  • Colle of American Pathologists recommends that ASCUS rate does not exceed SIL rate by 2-3x or drop to less than 5% of SIL rate

Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASCUS)

  • The general criteria for ASCUS is that of squamous cells with changes that exceed those of benign changes, but fall short of SIL
  • Changes fall short qualitatively: not all criteria are present
  • Changes fall short quantitatively: cells are few in number
  • ASCUS divided into two subtypes:
    • ASC-US, ASCUS of undetermined significance
    • ASC-H, ASCUS cannot rule out HSIL
  • ASC-US is defined as cytologic changes suggestive of LSIL but not definitive
  • ASC-H is defined as cytologic changes suggestive of HSIL but not definitive
  • Changes are also not definitively inflammation
  • ASC-US Criteria:
    • Increase in nuclear size, usually in mature squamous cells
    • Nucleus is 2.5-3 times that of an intermediate nucleus
    • Round to oval shape
    • Minimal membrane irregularities
    • Fine, evenly distributed chromatin, normal to mildly increased hyperchromasia
    • Cytoplasmic changes are minimal
    • Occasional bi-nucleation and peri-nuclear halos
    • Changes suggestive of koilocytes
    • These changes all fall short of SIL and/or there are cells with more definitive features but few in number
  • Patient management of AS-US is testing for HR-HPV types cervical cytology at 6 and 12 months
  • ASC-US is more variable than ASC-H
  • Variability can include:
    1. Cells with mature intermediate type cytoplasm
    2. Cells which have features of HPV such as rare cells with koilocytic changes
    3. Atypical (pleomorphic) parakeratotic changes
      • Identical to dyskeratocytes
      • APK are small cells with dense, eosinophilic to orangeophilic cytoplasm
      • Variable "atypical" nuclei
      • Elongated, enlarged and hyperchromatic
      • Mask an underlying significant lesion
    4. Atypical repair
      • Repair with nuclear abnormalities more than repair
      • Occurs in both squamous and endocervical cells
      • Crowding and pleomorphism
      • Irregular nuclear membranes
      • Dark and irregular chromatin
      • Rare single cells but overall cohesive
      • Clean background
    5. Atypia in atrophy: estrogen proliferation
      • Parabasal cells with big dark nuclei, high N:C ratio
      • Irregular nuclear membranes
      • Chromatin clumped and uneven
      • Hyperchromatic crowded groups
      • Dysplasia typically not found in atrophy (needs estrogen)
      • Estrogen test or colposcopy recommended

ASC-H

  • ASC-H is represented by changes in immature squamous metaplastic cells or reserve cells
  • These are immature cells with minimal cytoplasm and nuclei that are larger than squamous metaplastic cells
  • Increase in N:C ratio with some anisonucleolis, nuclear membrane irregularity, and hyperchromasia
  • The changes may fall short of definitive HSIL
  • It may be that there are few cells scattered on the slide quantitatively
  • Immature squamous cells: parasal and squamous metaplastic cytoplasm
  • Criteria:
    • Increase in nuclear size 2-3 times the size of normal squamous metaplastic cell nucleus
    • Slight irregular nuclear membrane
    • Slight increase in hyperchromasia
    • Irregular clumping of chromatin
    • Nigh N:C ratio
    • Thick dense cytoplasm
    • Isolated single cells in background should always be examined (often called "litigation cells")
    • Quantitatively innsufficient when less than 10 cells present
  • ASC-H also present in hyperchromatic crowded groups (HCG) or crowded sheets
    • HCG are often interpreted as AGUS
  • Differentials: carcinoma in situ, endocervical lesions, atrophic changes, and endometrial cells
  • Patient management for ASC-H colposcopy

Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion Development

  • Dysplasia is the impairment or partial impairment of basal cells as well as abnormal cell replication
  • The abnormal cells keep replicating and move up from these abnormal layers
  • It may present focally or multi-focally
  • Cells that exfoliate reflect changes in germinal cells
  • Classified by the thickness of epithelium with those impaired cells as well as the degree of abnormality in their nuclei
  • Mild dysplasia is found in the lower third of epithelium
  • Moderate dysplasia occupies between 1/3 and 2/3 of epithelium before maturation
  • Severe dysplasia occupies 2/3 of the epithelium before maturing
  • Carcinoma in situ has no maturation of basaloid cells
  • Overall Cytologic Pattern of Precursor Lesions:
    1. Increase in amonut of chromatin clumping (increases with severity)
    2. Wrinkling and deep indentation of the nucelar membrane
    3. Number of contacts between nuclear membrane and cytoplasmic border (increases with severity)
    4. Increase in nuclear size
    5. Increase in N:C ratio
    6. Increase in hyperchromasia
    7. Decrease in cytoplasm = decrease in maturation

Low Grade SIL (LSIL)

  • Most cases are associated with HR HPV 16
  • Also low risk types 6 and 11
  • Histologically recognized by the undifferentiated cells occupying the bottom one third of epithelium and minimal nuclear abnormality
    • These cells are found throughout tissue segments
  • Cells shed predominately in a single cell pattern (60% of cases)
  • Abnormal nuclear changes are observed in cells with mature cytoplasm
  • Polygonal shapes or round but with abundant cytoplasm
  • The cytoplasm is thick and dense, and may be basophilic or eosinophilic
  • Morphology
    • Central nuclei, single, bi-, or multi-nucleated
      • Consistent bi-nucleation may indicated HPV changes
    • Nuclei of mild dysplasia are the largest dysplastic nuclei
    • Size is 4-6 times the size of a normal intermediate cell nucleus
    • Lowest N:C ratio of the dysplasias
    • Nuclear membrane does not apporach cell border
    • Nuclear membrane wrinkling and invaginations are minimal
    • Hyperchromatic nuclei with coarsely granular chromatin (90% of cases)
    • No visible nucleoli on conventional preparation
  • Doctors can apply acetic acid (vinegar) to cervix, turning possible lesions white which will then be biopsied

Koilocytes

  • From the Greek term "koilos" meaning hollow"
  • It is an altered, usually mature squamous cell
  • Ultrastructural tests show viral particles within koilocytes
  • Large perinuclear cavity with central clearing and a dense peripheral rim of cytoplasm
  • Enlarged nuclei, commonly bi-nucleated
  • Chromatin is hyperchromatic, with mild chromatin clumping
  • Nuclear membrane shows slight irregularity
  • The cytoplasm may show increased eosinophilia and keratinization
  • In some cases, koilocytes may be present with minimal nuclear atypia
  • These cases should still be reported as LSIL
  • Differentials: peri-nuclear halos, de-glycogenated intermediate cells, reactive changes observed in Candida infections

Dyskeratocytes

  • Sheets and groups of squamous cells exhibiting very keratinized cytoplasm with small, hyperchromatic nuclei
  • The nucleus of these cells must be carefully examined for pleomorphism and irregular nuclear contours
  • They may be observed with or without koilocytic changes
  • Often reported as ASCUS
  • Differentials: hyperkeratosis and reactive changes

High Grade SIL (HSIL)

  • Includes moderate dysplasia (CIN 2), severe dysplasia and carcinoma in situ (CIN 3)
  • Cells have a greater capacity for progressing to invasive squamous cell carcinoma
  • Associated with HPV 16 and 18, and other types

Moderate Dysplasia

  • Undifferentiated basaloid cells occupy at least one third, but no more than two thirds of the epithelial thickness
  • Cells shed singly and in sheets and clusters
    • May also see syncytial groups
    • Shed a little more than LSIL, but sampling is still key
  • Round to oval, more intermediate to parabasal type than in LSIL
  • Cytoplasm is more cyanophilic than eosinophilic
  • Increase in the N:C ratio
  • Nucleus occupies less than one half of the cytoplasm and is enlarged
  • Hypercromatic
  • Nuclear membrane may approach cell membrane in one location
  • Increase chromatin coarseness and nuclear wrinkling
  • No prominent nucleoli

Severe Dysplasia

  • Two thirds of the epithelium is replaced with basaloid cells, but there is some squamous differentiation at the surface
  • Really no maturation of epithelium
  • Greater capacity for progressing to invasive carcinoma
  • Cell size comparable to parabasal cells, or smaller, may be spindly
  • Spare cytoplasm, a thin rim only
  • Nuclei contact cell membrane in more than one location
  • N:C ratio is high, nucleus occupies at least 2/3 of the cell
  • Cells shed singly, in aggregates or syncytial forms
  • Dense, hyperchromatic, coarsely granular chromatin
  • Evenly distributed
  • Nucleus is "raisinoid" with wrinkling and deep indentations of the membrane
  • Cytoplasm may have a degree of eosinophilia
  • Clean background

Carcinoma In Situ (CIS)

  • Entire thickness of the epithelium is replaced by immature cells
  • Cells are not found below the basement membrane
  • No squamous differentiation
  • Very high N:C ratio
  • Highest of the dysplasias
  • Cells are generally small
  • Round to oval shape
  • Cells presents predominately in syncytial, crowded 3D groups, loss of polarity
  • Single cell pattern may also observed
  • May be spindly in case of keratinizing dysplasia
  • Cells have pleomorphic "raisinoid" nuclei, hyperchromatic with coarsely granular, evenly distributed chromatin
  • Nuclei may become elongated
  • Very sparse cytoplasm
  • Cells may be numerous, again depends on sampling
  • Mitotic figures/apoptotic bodies may be found
  • Clean background, look for patches of tumor diathesis
  • Differentials: Endometrial cells and endocervical cells

Types of Carcinoma in Situ

  • Carcinoma In Situ is the most clinically significant of the high grade SILs
  • Carcinoma in Situ is NOT cancer but has the highest risk of developing to invasive cancer
  • Morphologically three types of CIS exist
    • Small cell CIS
    • Intermediate cell CIS
    • Large cell CIS
  • Recognized best on histology
  • Types not reported cytologically
  • No difference in treatment or prognosis

CIS-Small Cell

  • Undifferentiated small abnormal cells
  • Measure 11um in diameter
  • Present in HGC or syncytia
  • Oval to spindly nuclei
  • High mitotic rate
  • Hyperchromatic chromatin, fine to coarse and evenly distributed
  • Chromocenters and no nucleoli

CIS-Intermediate Cell

  • Medium sized cells
  • HCG or syncytia
  • Round to oval nuclei about 2-3.5 times the size of intermediate cell nuclei
  • Twice the size of metaplastic cell nuclei
  • Hyperchromatic with predominately fine, occasionally coarse chromatin
  • Nucleoli more prominent

CIS-Large Cell

  • Least common type of CIS
  • More cytoplasm than other CIS
  • Lower N:C ratio
  • Can present as naked nuclei
  • Challenging pattern cytologically
  • Delicate, cyanophilic, ill-defined cytoplasm
  • Nuclei 4-6 times the size or normal intermediate cell nuclei
  • Syncytial arrangements, rarely single cells
  • Irregular membranes
  • Hyperchromatic chromatin, fine with chromocenters
  • Inconspicuous nucleoli

Endocervical Gland Extension

  • Carcinoma is defined when basement membrane is intact
  • Has propensity to grow into endocervical glands (endocervical gland extension)
  • Often times cells from squamous carcinoma in situ extend to the endocervical glands
  • Cells will have dysplastic, elongated nuclei
  • Cytoplasmic features such as vacuolization and basophilia
  • Cells will be found admixed with normal endocervical cells

Microinvasion

  • Isolated cells from CIS have infiltrated the basement membrane
  • Histologic diagnosis, cytology can only provide clues
  • Large numbers of cells in syncytia and sheets
  • Single cells with features of carcinoma
  • Background contains patches of tumor diathesis (key)
  • Isolated nucleoli in metaplastic dysplasia (potentially good clue)
  • Report: CIS suggest microinvasion

Morphologic Forms of Dysplasia

  • Morphologically type types of dysplasia
    • Keratinized dysplasia
    • Metaplastic dysplasia
  • Arises in, and mimics, benign protective mechanisms
    • Keratosis
    • Squamous metaplasia

Metaplastic Dysplasia

  • Dysplastic cells associated with squaous metaplasia
  • Dysplastic nuclei are found in squamous metaplastic cells
  • Described as mature and immature types
  • Indicates that lesions has extended or has arisen in the transformation
  • Cells are classified as HSIL
    • Mature squamous metaplasia can be more like LSIL
  • Cells are mainly single but also in loose sheets
  • Single cell pattern is hard to find
  • Parabasal type cytoplasm but may be polygonal
  • Hyperchromatic nuclei with fine chromatin
  • 4-6 times the size of normal intermediate cell nuclei
  • Always examine nuclei of metaplastic cells with care
  • Challenge to differentiate from normal squamous metaplasia (bland nuclei are main clue)
  • Nuclear membrane irregularites are subtle, HSIL is challenging to diagnose
  • Differentials: LSIL, reactive changes, histiocytes

Keratinizing Dysplasia

  • Also know as pleomorphic dysplasia
  • Challenging to grade as low or high grade
  • Atypical changes in mature superficial layer or the stratified squamous epithelium
    • Occurs in the external os of the cervix
  • Associated with hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis and APK
  • Abnormal superficial and intermediate-type squamous cells
  • Low grade keratinizing dysplasia, often associated with HPV and dyskeratocytes
  • Single cells, sheets, pearl formation, tadpole, bizarre forms
  • Polygonal or square cells are present 50% of the time
  • Abundant cytoplasm, heavily keratinized and orangeophilic
  • Single or multiple nuclei, centrally located
  • Enlarged, twice the size of normal cell type
  • Variation in shape of nuclei
  • Nuclear membrane is well-defined, smooth in 60% of cases, wrinkled the other 40%
  • Keratinizing dysplasia is difficult to differentiate from invasive squamous cell carcinoma
  • Often reported as: "SIL cannot rule out invasion" or "HSIL cannot rule out invasion"
  • Main clue is clean background with no tumor diathesis

Challenges

  • Pregnancy
    • Atypias of pregnancy should not deter one from diagnosing SILs
    • The hormones of pregnancy can accelerate the development of SILs
    • Pregnancy changes can also mimic SILs
    • Increase in nuclear size and hyperchromasia, more prominent nucleoli
    • Cells may be more numerous
    • Background is dirty
  • Post-Partum
    • Reparative changes may be observed along with a background of parabasal cells
    • Should be conservative, ask for estrogen proliferation test and repeat if cells suspicious for carcinoma in situ are noted
  • Postmenopausal Atrophy
    • Very difficult to differentiate
    • Cells from CIS occur in sheets and appear similar to sheets of atrophic cells
    • Both have dirty background, both have irregular nuclei, same staining reaction
    • Cells resemble parabasal and immature cells
    • Estrogen therapy should be suggested before making a final diagnosis
