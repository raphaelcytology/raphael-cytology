Proliferative and Benign Conditions

  • These conditions are benign in nature
  • Cytologic pattern ranges from distinct to non-specific
  • Derive from change in pH, hormones, trauma, polyps
  • Some of these conditions are rarely, if ever, seen
  • Reporting in 2001/2014 TBS under "Other Non-Neoplastic Findings", which are optional to report
  • Reporting varies with each laboratory

Squamous Metaplasia

  • Vaginal canal-fetus development squamous epithelium grows from urogenital sinus
  • Covers columnar glandular epithelium derived from Mullerian origin
  • Replaces glandular epithelium, becomes native squamous epithelium
  • Puberty and pregnancy cause eversion of endocervical glands termed ectropion onto portio vaginalis
    • More apparent on anterior lip of cervix
    • Response to vaginal acidity, trauma, stimulus, polyps
    • Everted endocervical glands undergo process of squamous metaplasia
    • More common during fetal development, puberty, pregnancy
    • Forms a new squamocolumnar junction (T-zone) which changes throughout life
  • At reproductive age T-zone is distal to os, moves up endocervix with increased age
  • Important: Site of squamous and glandular carcinomas
  • Common protective mechanism of the cervix
    • Like a callus
  • It is the main protective mechanism of the endocervix and transformation zone
  • Metaplasia is the transformation of cell type to another, in this case glandular cells try to become like squamous cells
  • It is reverisble, however metaplastic cells have a greater degree of malignant transformation than normal cells
  • Squamous metaplastic cells represent T-zone sampling
    • Ten indicate T-zone sampling adequacy
  • Squamous metaplasia begins with endocervical reserve cells of Mullerian origin
  • Reserve cell hyperplasia begins the process
  • Endocervical reserve cells can differentiate into squamous and endocervical cells
  • Reserve cell hyperplasia leads to immature squamous metaplasia
    • Then becomes mature squamous metaplasia

Reserve Cell Hyperplasia

  • Reserve cells proliferate along basement membrane
  • Reserve cell create 2-5 layers of undifferentiated cells
    • Best identified on biopsy
  • Rare to recognize on cytology samples
  • Pattern is syncytial aggregates to sheet of immature cells
  • Indistinguishable from histiocytes or stromal cells
  • Round cells very scanty cytoplasm, vacuolated and cyanophilic
  • Single nucleus (50 um2) with high N:C ratio
  • Fine chromatin with chromocenters
  • Differential: ASC-H or HSIL

Immature and Mature Metaplasia

  • Immature squamous metaplsia is the type of metaplasia that evolves from reserve cell hyperplasia
    • This is recognized cytologically
  • Mature squamous metaplasia is a histologic term describing squamous epithelium overlying endocervical glands
  • We use the terms immature and mature squamous metaplasia
  • Cells develop features of mature, non-keratinizing squamous epithelium
Immature Squamous Metaplasia
  • Reserve cells begin acquire squamous features
  • Key cytologic features:
    • Size of parabasal cells, vary in size
    • Arranged in singly, loose sheets and cobblestone pattern
    • Dense cytoplasm and distinct borders
    • Round cytoplasm, thick and dense
    • Spider-like extensions, dense ectoplasmic rim underneath cell membrane
    • Vacuoles with neutrophils common
    • Nucleus measures 50um2
    • Round to oval, smooth borders, fine chromatin and chromocenters
    • Prominent nucleoli are relatively common
Mature Squamous Metaplasia
  • Key cytologic features:
    • Cytoplasm denser due to increased development of cytoskeleton (keratin)
    • Intercellular junctions are more evident
    • Cell membrane more developed
    • Cytologic features appear as intermediate cells
    • Cobblestone pattern remains
    • Shape is more round polygonal, with vacuoles
    • Denser cytoplasm
    • Nucleus is single, large, central, and vesicular
    • Nucleoli range from inconspicuous to prominent
    • Cells are more polygonal in shape and mold
    • One end may flatten (reminiscence of terminal bar)

Repair and Regenerative Changes

  • Federal regulation requires all cases of reactive/regenerative changes to be referred to pathologists
  • Erosion or ulceration of the cervix is caused by trauma or acute inflammation
  • Repair begins immediately following the injury to fill in the defect
    • This process is called re-epithelization
  • Peripheral tissue proliferates to fix damage
    • Rapid cell regeneration and protein synthesis
  • Changes are observed in patient's following childbirth, surgery, and treatment
    • Also in patients with Bechet's disease (genital ulcers)
  • Repair observed in all cell types
  • Main differential is neoplastic cells
  • Repair occurs predominately in flat sheets (50%) which are cohesive
  • In malignancy, isolated malignant cells will be present
  • Cytologic pattern:
    • Cells are large and exhibit pseudopod-like cytoplasmic projections
    • These projections tend to "flow" in one direction giving a "school of fish" appearance
    • The cytoplasm may be basophilic or eosinophilic
    • The nucleus is vesicular with finely to coarsely granular, evenly distributed chromatin that tends to be pale
    • There is slight pleomorphism in the nuclei, but one very important feature is the maintenance of polarity
    • The nuclear membrane tends to be heavy and nuclear streaming may be observed
    • Can increase 2-3 times in size
    • Mitosis may be observed, multinucleation is also possible
    • Cells are more rounded on LBP
  • One of the most important diagnostic features is the presence of a large, spherical and prominent nucleolus which tends to be single in squamous cells and multiple in endocervical cells
  • The background has a variable number of inflammatory cells, protein deposists, fresh and old blood, and fibroblasts
  • Squamous metaplasia can be mixed with repair
  • Differential is malignant cells
Main clues:
  • Repair is orderly and cohesive, no single cells
  • Chromatin remains fine and even, hypochromatic
  • Nuclear borders are smooth
  • All nucleoli are prominent
  • Adequate amount of cytoplasm, not decreased

Atrophy

  • Atrophy is the result of a deficiency in both estrogen and progesterone production
  • It is commonly observed in postmenopausal and post-partum women and under certain hormonal conditions
  • The vaginal epithelium of a female with atrophy is thin and tough
  • Specimens are often scanty
  • Cytologic pattern is predominately of parabasal cells exfoliating singly or scraped in sheets
  • These sheets may take on a syncytial pattern mimicking changes of HSIL
  • Chromatin is finely granular
  • Cytoplasm of parabasal cells may be vacuolated and slight pleomorphism may be noted in the nucleus and cytoplasm
  • Nucleus may demonstrate clumped chromatin
  • Smears may appear "air-dried" with some cells having eosinophilic cytoplasm
  • Air-drying artifact also causes artifical nuclear enlargement
  • Cell may fuse together, forming a type of multi-nucleated giant cell
  • Occasionally, tiny round parabasal cells with keratinized cytoplasm and pyknotic nuclei may be observed
    • These nuclear changes may mimic the hyperchromatic changes observed in dysplasia
  • Transitional cell metaplasia in which prominent longitudinal nuclear grooves, wrinkled nuclei and small per-nuclear halos can also mimic abnormal changes
  • The endocervical cells become cuboidal with more visualization of cilia, less secretory vacuoles and numerous stripped nuclei
  • In atrophic conditions, TBS does not require the reporting of the presence or absence of endocervical cells
  • Background consists of granular protein deposists and may contain "blue blobs"
  • Be careful, this background is similar to that observed in invasive carcinoma
  • When in doubt, an estrogen proliferation test should be performed
  • Atrophy in the presence of variable inflammation is classified as atrophic vaginitis
  • This background also mimics that of invasive carcinoma
  • Some labs will consistently report the pattern of atrophic vaginitis

    • Transitional Cell Metaplasia

    • Replacement of normal squamous epithelium with urothelium (epithelium of lower urinary tract)
    • Associated with atrophy
    • Histology: 10 layers of hyperplastic epithelium
    • Cytologic appearance:
      • Streaming appearance
      • No picket fence
      • Squamous cells resemble umbrella cells
      • Oval, elongated, streaming nuclei
      • Longitudinal nuclear grooves
      • Hyperchromatic, even chromatin
      • Differential: HSIL

    Keratotic Changes

    Hyperkeratosis

    • This is a protective increase in the thickness of he squamous epithelium due to irritation and/or injury
      • Two additional layers
    • Caused by uterine prolapse and pessary use, inflammation, and atrophy
    • Can also be associated with dysplasia and carcinoma (keratinized)
    • Clinically, it presents as leukoplakia
      • White plaques on the surface epithelium that cannot be removed on scraping
      • The differential is Candida, which can be removed with scraping
    • Cytologic pattern is that of sheets of anucleated squamous cells with heavily keratinized cytoplasm which may appear as ground glass
    • Squamous pearls may also be noted
      • This condition can be associated with dysplasia and carcinoma especially if... >

    Parakeratosis

    • This condition is associated with chronic irritation and is observed in benign and malignant conditions
    • Similar to hyperkeratosis, associated with benign and malignant conditions
      • More frequently associate with...
    • Cytologic pattern is that of small, round cells with heavily keratinized cytoplasm
    • These cells may be singly or in sheets (which can be challenging to diagnose)
    • Cells have a single pyknotic nucleus
    • Screen carefully for abnormal nuclei, these changes are referred to as atypical parakeratosis ("APK cells")
      • If found, these changes should be reported as an ECA

    Follicular Cervicitis

    • Lymphoid follicle formation with germinal centers
    • More common in older women
    • Commonly associated with Chlamydia infection
    • Pattern is similar to reactive lymph node
      • Numerous lymphocytes and occasional plasma cells
    • Range in maturation (size)
      • Immature lymphocytes are larger
    • Tingible body macrophages may be noted
      • These contain cellular debris in cytoplasm
    • Capillaries noted
    • In LBP: appear as hyperchromatic crowded groups, lymphocytes are dispersed
    • Differentials: endometrial cells and HSIL
    • Primary lymphoma of cervix is rare

    Intrauterine Device (IUD)

    • It is critical that information of IUD usage is provided
    • IUD causes changes that are similar to carcinoma
    • It is known to cause infection in the endometrium which in turn produces reactive changes in endometrial and endocervical cells
    • Insertion of IUD results in shedding of endometrial cells and histiocytes
      • Histiocytes may contain sperm
    • The endometrial cells appear singly and in clusters with scanty cytoplasm but with little pleomorphism
    • Uniform nuclei, may be degenerated with clumped chromatin
    • The cytoplasm may contain degenerative vacuoles
    • Inflammatory cells may be observed in the background
    • Endocervical/metaplastic cells shed in papillary aggregates
    • Endocervical cells become large and distended with secretory vacuoles, giving the cells a "bubble-gum" appearance
    • Nuclei enlarge and contain macronucleoli while nuclear membrane remains smooth
    • Common finding with IUD usage is the presence of Actinomyces bacteria
    • IUD changes differ from adenocarcinoma by lack of pleomorphism and the presence of smooth nuclear membrane and even distribution of chromatin
    • Less common IUD change is the presence of small, isolated cells with dark, smudged nuclei and smooth nuclear borders mimicking HSIL
    • These cells are believed to be degenerated endocervical/endometrial cells
    • HSIL cells tend to be more abundant with coarse chromatin and irregular nuclear membranes

    Rectovaginal/Vesicovaginal Fistulas

    • Tract formation between rectum-vagina, and bladder-vagina
    • Associated with:
      • Colorectal cancers extending into vagina
      • Chron's disease and surgery
    • Fistulas associated with malignancy contain cancer cells
      • Can be found in fecal material
    • Associated with benign process
      • Tall columnar colorectal goblet cells
    • Vesicovaginal fistulas are similar process
      • There can be urine in the vaginal canal
      • Can see degenerated urothelial cells

    Therapy Effect

    • Clinical history of radiation is key
    • Various therapies are used to treat inflammatory conditions, benign and malignant neoplasms
    • In the female genital tract, lesions can be excised with a knife, via electrocautery, laser, or cryosurgery
    • Radiation, immunosuppressive drugs and chemotherapy are used to stop the spread of neoplastic
    • Changes from all treatments are observed in both normal and abnormal cells

      • Surgical Treatments

      • Common surgical procedures include:
        • Biopies
        • Laser therapy
        • Cryotherapy
        • Electrodiathermy or LEEP (loop electrocautery excision procedures)
      • Changes depend on extent of surgical process as well as time since surgery
      • Immediately post-op: smears contain tissue fragments, inflammatory cells, blood, nectroic debris, and degenerative changes
      • One week post-op: reparative changes with prominent nucleoli, multi-nucleated giant cells and cell debris
      • Leads to tubal metaplasia and cone artifact

        • Laser and LEEP

        • Done in conization of cervix-treatment and diagnosis of HSIL
        • Heat artifact
        • Elongated and spindly squamous cells
        • Elongated nuclei and cytoplasm
        • Smudgy, degenerated chromatin
        • Endocervical cells appear thin and pencil-like

        Chemotherapy

        • TBS no longer requires reporting of chemtherapeutic changes
        • Alkylating chemicals: inhibit DNA/RNA production
        • Systemic drugs do not cause changes in cells
        • Busulfan: systemic drug may produce changes similar to cancer
        • Topical agents will show changes
          • Ex. Podophyllin: used to treat condylomas
        • Changes observed mimic those observed in radiation, and in dysplastic changes
        • May produce atrophy of squamous epithelium

        Immunosuppressive Drugs

        • May give rise to neoplastic changes
        • No specific changes are observed in the cells

        Radiation Therapy

        • Decision on radiotherapy is determined by type and stage of cancer
        • Cell changes in response to radiation depends on:
          1. Type of cell
          2. How sensitive it is to radiation
          3. Type and amount of radiation used
        • Hypoxic cells are radioresistant
        • Most sensitive cells are those that are rapidly reproducing, high mitotic rate ex. (bone marrow, hair, intestinal cells, and carcinoma)
        • Radiation causes rapid cell death, inhibits cell division and alters chromosomal and genetic information
        • Cells are most sensitive during M phase and G2 phase
        • Cells are most resistant during DNA synthesis and S phase
        • Moderately resistant after DNA systhesis
        • Cells with high turnover are sensitive
        • Types of radiation used include radium, x-ray, and cobalt
        • Cells, benign and malignant, begin to shed within one day of radiation
        • Response differs by patient
        • Changes may appear for a lifetime
        • Radiation changes are exhibited in the cytoplasm and nucleus

        Brachytherapy

        • Localized radiation treatment/seeding
        • Cervical cancer treated with intracavitary bracytherapy
        • High and low dose radiation

        Acute Radiation Changes

        • Severe inflammatory response
        • Tissue death up to 8 weeks
        • Histologically:
          • Tissue degeneration and necrosis
          • Vascular damage
          • Erythema and edema
          • Desquamation and sloughing off of tissue
          • Changes in connective tissue and vessels
          • Regenerative changes
        • Changes with acute radiation are unsatisfactory
          • Severe diathesis of WBC, blood, and degenerative changes
          • Challenging to find diagnostic malignant cells
        • Nuclear changes:
          • Nuclear hypertrophy, nucleus increases 2-10 times
          • No change in N:C ratio
          • Multi-nucleation common
          • Pale chromatin pattern
          • Irregular shape, nuclear molding and wrinkling
          • No thickening of nuclear membrane
          • Peri-nuclear halos surround nuclei
          • Vacuolization (cell death), karyorrhexis
          • Nucleoli enlarge, may be multiple, regular in shape
        • Cytoplasmic changes:
          • Cytomegaly is hallmark (macrocytes)
            • 200 um
            • Edema and hypertrophy- cells increase in size 3-4 times
            • No real change in N:C ratio
          • Cytoplasm becomes dense amphophilic, eosinophilic, and polychromatic
          • Thin hair-like structures are noted in the cytoplasm
          • Vacuolization, starting small to progessively large, thick borders with no inclusions
          • Pseudocannibalism: cells appear to engulf one another
          • In later stages, cytopalsmic fragmentation
        • Background:
          • Large numbers of PMNs and histiocytes, FBGs
          • Histiocytes may contain debris in cytoplasm
          • Reparative changes (spider cells) are commonly observed
          • Necrotic cell debris often mixed with mucus
          • Squamous and intermediate cells may contain PMNs in their cytoplasm
          • If ovaries are radiated, atrophic pattern
        • Glandular cells have distortion of clusters and honeycomb pattern
          • Cytomegaly
          • Vacuolization
          • Enlarged, multiple nuclei, hyperchromatic
          • N:C ratio remains the same
        • Stromal cells also have macrocytic change
          • Cytoplasmic extensions (octopus-like)
        • Large dark nuclei
        • Reparative changes can be extremely challenging
        • Look for features of repair: cohesion, flowing cytoplasm, order
        • Nuclear and cytoplasmic features become bizarre

        Chronic Radiation Changes

        • Change lasting 6 months to a year
        • Changes are same as in acute phase
        • Background is cleaner or similar to atrophy
        • Scanty cellularity common, lack of endocervical/metaplastic cells
        • Macrocytes persist
        Residual Carcinoma
        • Paptests typically taken 8 weeks after radiation
        • Cancer cells take 6-8 weeks to clear
        • Goal: looking for residual cancer cells, same criteria as malignant cells
        • High N:C ratio, crisp chromatin
        • Most cancer cells are biologically inactive
        • Mitotic figures indicate active
        • If persistent, active cancer cells, poor prognosis
        Irradiated Malignant Lesions
        • Cancer cells shed in large numbers, singly, clusters, and sheets right after treatment
        • Cancer cells will have the same radiation changes as benign cells
        • Resemble normal cancer cells
        • Main clue will be higher N:C ratio in malignant cells
        • Thickened nuclear membrane in cancer cells
        • Crisp chromatin in malignant cells
        • Nucleoli same in both
        • Abnormal mitotic figures in malignancy
        Persistent and Recurrent Carcinoma
        • Cytologically challenging pattern
        • Peristent: radiation was not effective
          • Small cell carcinoma cells
          • 8 week Pap: Malignant cells with radiation change indicate they were present but not destroyed
          • Mitosis means viability
        • Recurrent: complete response but came back
          • Malignant cells with no radiation change indicate they recurred after treatment
        • Clinical presentation and biopsy key
        Post Radiation Dysplasia
        • Radiation causes mutations and can cause carcinoma
        • 20% of treated women may develop
        • Post radiation dysplasia develops 6 months to decades later
        • Poor prognosis if within 3 years of treatment
        • Risk factor for residual carcinoma
        • Patients with HPV+ increased risk
        • Cytologic pattern of dysplasia, keratinizing type
        • High N:C ratio
        • Small cell dysplasia associated with atrophy
        • Persistent estrogen effect increases risk of dysplasia
