Cytologic Findings

Squamous Cells

Main cells of focus when screening samples from the vagina and cervix. They will make up the majority of cells in the sample and are not routinely dotted. The types are derived from the layers: basal, parabasal, intermediate, and superficial.

Basal Cells

  • Derived from the stratum cylindricum, the cell layer right above the basement membrane.
  • Also known as germinal cells, anchor epithelium to basement membrane.
  • Replenish mucosa and are the only cells in squamous mucosa to divide.
  • Daughter cells undergo squamous differentiation.
  • Rarely seen in cervical vaginal smears, only in case of extreme erosion and atrophy.
Morphology
  • Exfoliate in sheets or tight clusters
  • Measure 10-12um in diameter
  • Oval to round in shape with round to oval nucleus
  • Fine chromatin with chromocenters
  • Resemble small histiocytes

Parabasal Cells

  • Derived from the stratum spinosum.
  • More commonly observed in ulceration and atrophic conditions.
  • Atrophic conditions commonly observed in childhood and postmenopausal years (associated with lack of hormone production).
  • Larger than basal cells.
Morphology
  • Occur in sheets, syncytia or as single cells
  • Measure 15-30um in diameter, nuclei measure 8-9 um
  • Cells and nuclei are round to oval
  • Nuclei are central and single, occasionally multinucleated
  • Cytoplasm is thick, translucent green to blue
  • N:C ratio is moderately high, 1:2
  • Chromatin pattern is finely granular with even distribution
  • Occasional prominent nucleoli may be noted

Intermediate Cells

  • Derived from the middle layer of stratified squamous epithelium.
  • They, along with superficial cells, are most commonly observed cells in Paps.
  • Most often seen in post-ovulatory, pregnant and menopausal women.
Morphology
  • Commonly occur singly, but clump under certain hormonal conditions
  • Measure 35-50um in diameter, nuclei measure 7-8um (nucleus often used for measuring other cells)
  • Thin, semi-transparent cytoplasm which typically stains blue
  • Polygonal shape with a slight curl to cell edges
  • Pale yellow glycogen may be noted, depending on hormonal status
  • N:C ratio is low, 2:10
  • Fine chromatin with smooth distribution (nucleus is vesicular, "viable")
  • Rare nucleoli that are tiny and inconspicuous
Barr Bodies
  • Mass of condensed chromatin in nuclei of normal female somatic cells
  • Represents the inactivated X chromatin
    • 1 of 2 X chromosomes normally
    • Inactivation occurs on day 16 of embryonic development
    • Barr bodies may be seen attached to the inner lining of the nuclear membrane
    • Tiny 1um chromatin attached at any point
    • Numerous bodies indicates a genetic disorder and neoplasia
Navicular Cells
  • The term navicular means "boat-shaped"
  • Intermediate cells take on a triagular, elongated form
  • Their cytoplasm is rich with yellow glycogen
  • Glycogen is a normal component of the cytoplasm of squamous cells but more commonly observed in intermediate cells
  • Commonly found in pregnancy/postmenopausal women
  • Appears as yellow-brown pigment
  • When removed, leaves a halo/hollow space that may mimic HPV-related koilocyte

Superficial Cells

  • Originate from stratified corneum.
  • Most common epithelial cell seen in pre-ovulatory cytle.
  • Nucleus begins to degenerate and shrink, cytoplasm increases.
Morphology
  • Occur singly
  • Measure 45-50um in diameter, nuclei measure approxiamtely 5um
  • Wafer thin, transparent cytoplasm with no folding of the edges
  • Cytoplasm typically stains pink, yellow to blue
  • Brown-black lipofuscin granules may be found in cytoplasm
  • N:C ratio is very low, 1:10
  • No chromatin detail is appreciated, nucleus is dead
  • Rare nucleoli that are tiny and inconspicuous
Squamous Pearls
  • These structures are an incidental finding and are not routinely reported
  • They form when squamous cells fall in to an epithelial pit and are compressed
  • Mixture of superficial, intermediate, and parabasal cells
  • Concentric ring of cells around a central cell
  • May be seen in association with benign or malignant processes
Anucleated Cells
  • These are an occasional finding
  • Well-preserved superficial cell cytoplasm with no nuclei
  • Cytoplasm may or may not be keratinized (orangeophilic)
  • Shadow or "ghost" of a nuclei may be found in center of cytoplasm
  • Large numbers may indicate hyperkeratosis which is a benign protective mechanism
  • Cells are identical to squamous cells from skin

Glandular Cells

Glandular cells are the next type of epithelial cell that are observed in cervical vaginal smears. They are not as frequently observed as squamous cells. The two main types are from the endocervical canal and endometrium cavity. Cells from the fallopian tubes and ovaries are very rarely encountered.

Endocervical Cells

  • Most commonly observed columnar cell.
  • Must be marked as part of The Bethesda System adequacy requirement for transition zone sampling.
  • They secrete protein and mucus.
    • Consists of mucopolysaccharides and sulfated sialic acid.
    • Estrogen promote production while progesterone inhibits.
    • Mucin production is alkaline, while normal pH is acidic
Morphology
  • Can be seen as clusters, honeycomb sheets, palisade and singly
  • Semi-transparent lacy cytoplasm which stains moderately blue and can be vacuolated
  • Measure 20-30um height and 8-10um wide
  • Nuclear size is 9-20um and N:C ratio is 3:10
  • Cell borders are usually indistinct
  • Chromatin is fine to coarsely granular and hyperchromatic, often with small, red nucleoli

Endometrial Glandular Cells

  • Commonly observed during menstrual cycle, but also during pregnancy, IUD usage, and disease process.
  • May be observed due to exfoliation or forcibly removed via aspiration, scraping, washing
  • Often seen spontaneously exfoliated during the first 12 days of menstrual cycle.
  • Must be marked and reported in women above 45.
  • Can also bee seen in pattern of exodus (glandular and stromal components) or lower uterine segment.
Morphology
  • Found in tight clusters, tighter than endocervical cells
  • Round, measuring 10-15um
  • Scant, wispy, translucent blue cytoplasm, may be finely vacuolated or granular
  • Rare cilia may be seen
  • Nuclei are coarse with even chromatin clumping and hyperchromatic
Endometrial Stromal Cells
  • Derived from the spongiosa (deep) layer or the compact (superficial) layer of the endometrium
  • Two types:
    • Superficial: Stromal histiocytes, clean up debris, cannot distinguish from other histiocytes, more commonly observed day 6-10 of cycle
    • Deep
  • Superficial stromal cells are found in loose clusters or as single cells
  • They have adequate vacuolated, cyanophilic cytoplasm with ill-defined borders
    • Phagocytosed material occasionally in cytoplasm
  • Nuclei are single and eccentric with a granular chromatin pattern
  • Look alikes or differentials are:
    • Atypical glandular cells
    • High grade squamous epithelial lesion cells
  • Deep stromal cellsresemble lymphocytes or small histiocytes but contain no phagocytized debris
  • Small, spindle or stellate cells (radiating, star-like pattern)
  • Scant cyanophilic to amphphilic cytoplasm
  • Oval to spindle-shaped nuclei
    • Longitudinal groove in nuclear membrane
  • May be mistaken for cells from a high grade squamous lesion
    • Forcibly Removed Endometrial Cells
    • Endocerivcal sampling devices sometimes samples endometrial cells from the lower uterine segment
    • These endometrial cells differ in appearance than endometrial cells that are exfoliated
    • These cells present in large and small tissue fragments consisting of endometrial glandular and stromal cells
    • The groups often have elongated, tubular shapes
    • The cells resemble endcervical cells but with high N:C ratios
    • They may be mistaken for squamous or glandular lesions

    Fallopian Tube Cells

    • Rarely seen, resemble endocervical cells but with denser cytoplasm.
    • Three types: Ciliated, Secretory, Peg-shaped
    • Ciliated cells have round to oval nuclei with granular cytoplasm.
    • Cilia stains red ad is attached to terminal bar.
    • Secretory cells contain foamy cytoplasm that stains pink to green.
    • Single eccentric nuclei may contain multiple small nucleoli.

    Nonepithelial Cells

    Smooth Muscle Cells

    • Rarely seen, may be observed in cases of trauma and ulceration.
    • Elongated cells with spindly, elongated cytoplasm.

    Red Blood Cells (Erythrocytes)

    • Biconcave, round, anucleated structures.
    • Measures 6-7um, stains yellow-orange, green-blue.
    • May be found in the cytoplasm of macrophages.
    • Can be used as ruler for measuring surrounding cells.

    Polymorphonuclear Leukocytes

    • Type of white blood cells (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils)
    • Measure 10um
    • Inflammatory cells primarily associated with acute inflammation
    • Nuclei are lobulated, attached by thread of chromatin material.

    Lymphocytes

    • Range in size: small, medium, large
    • Usually slightly larger than RBC
    • Found in chronic inflammatory conditions
    • High N:C ratio and nuclei is hyperchromatic and slightly wrinkled
    • Nucleoli not usually present in benign conditions

    Plasma Cells

    • Observed in chronic conditions
    • Oval shaped cell, measuring 8-12 um
    • Eccentric, round to oval single nucleus
    • Chromatin is coarse, giving nucleus a "cartwheel" appearance
    • "Plasmacytoid features" often used to describe cells with eccentric nuclei

    Macrophages / Histiocytes

    • Both monocytes, macrophages are most mature
    • Small (10-15um) to large, multinucleated (15-120um)
    • They have phagocytic properties
    • Found in chronic inflammatory conditions

    Fibrocytes / Fibroblasts

    • May be observed in post-abortion or post-delivery patients
    • Derived from granulation tissue observed in healing process
    • Elongated spdinly cells that can mimic squamous cell carcinoma

    Contaminants

    Sperm

    • Grey to blue structure with or without a tail
    • Head is oval to triangular

    Pollen

    • Appearance depends on species
    • Common in Spring and Summer
      • Transparent, glassy capsule, cytoplasm may be granular and orangeophilic, mimicking carcinoma

    Lubricant

    • Deep blue/purple masses, denser than mucus
    • Can resemble bacteria
    • May plug filter in ThinPrep processing, resulting in scanty cells

    Talcum

    • Transparent with "Matlese Cross" under polarized light

    Crystalline Bodies

    • Hematoidin Crystals or "Cockleburs"
    • Probably degenerated blood product
    • Golden brown refractile masses

    Cornflaking (Xylene Artifact)

    • Brown obscuring artifact resulting from the evaporation of xylene and the trapping of airbubbles during coverslipping
    • Usually observed on mature squamous cells
